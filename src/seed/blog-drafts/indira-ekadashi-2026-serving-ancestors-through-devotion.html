During the dark fortnight of the month of Ashvin, many families across India perform rituals for departed ancestors. This period, popularly known as Pitru Paksha, is a time when people remember parents and grandparents who have passed away and offer prayers for their wellbeing. Right in the middle of this fortnight falls Indira Ekadashi, a fast day whose story speaks directly to the question every grieving heart asks: is there anything I can still do for those I have lost?

According to the ISKCON Vaishnava calendars we consulted, Indira Ekadashi is observed on 6 October 2026. Fasting rules and the exact time for breaking the fast (parana) the next morning vary by location, so please check the Vaishnava calendar for Visakhapatnam timings.

The Story of King Indrasena

The history of this Ekadashi is traditionally recounted from the Brahma-vaivarta Purana, in a conversation where Lord Krishna describes its glories to Maharaja Yudhishthira.

In ancient times a pious king named Indrasena ruled the city of Mahishmati. He was devoted to Lord Vishnu, cared for his subjects, and lived a prosperous life. One day the sage Narada Muni came to his court with a message. During a visit to the abode of Yamaraja, Narada had met the king's late father, who was suffering there because in his life he had broken an Ekadashi vow. The father had sent a request through Narada: if his son would observe the Ekadashi of the dark fortnight of Ashvin and offer its merit to him, he would be released.

Indrasena was moved. Following Narada's instructions, he and his family observed the fast with care. They worshipped the Lord, stayed awake through the night in remembrance of Him, honoured the brahmanas and broke the fast properly the next day. As a result, the story tells, his father was freed and ascended to the spiritual realm. Indrasena himself continued ruling righteously and at the end of his life also attained the Lord's abode.

What the story is really about

At first glance this is a story about rituals for the dead. Read more carefully, it is about the extraordinary potency of devotional service. The father's release came not from a mechanical ceremony but from his son's sincere service to Lord Vishnu. That service benefited not only the son but his whole family line.

Ancestors and the Vaishnava Understanding

The Bhagavad-gita shows that concern for ancestors is a natural part of Vedic culture. In the first chapter (Bg. 1.41), Arjuna worries that if family traditions are destroyed, the offerings of food and water to the forefathers will stop. In his purport Srila Prabhupada explains that the standard practice was to offer such food first to Lord Vishnu, so that the forefathers receive prasadam, which can free them from suffering.

The Srimad-Bhagavatam takes this further. It states (SB 11.5.41) that one who has fully taken shelter of Mukunda, Lord Krishna, is no longer a debtor to the demigods, sages, ordinary living beings, family members or forefathers. This is not because such a person is ungrateful, but because complete devotion to the Lord automatically satisfies every obligation, just as watering the root of a tree nourishes all its branches.

The example of Prahlada

When Lord Nrisimhadeva offered Prahlada Maharaja any benediction he wanted, Prahlada asked only that his father Hiranyakashipu, who had tried to kill him, be forgiven. The Lord replied that not only his father but twenty-one generations of his family were already purified because of Prahlada's devotion (SB 7.10.18). One sincere devotee in a family is the greatest gift that family can receive.

Why Ekadashi Matters So Much

Ekadashi, the eleventh day of each lunar fortnight, is called Hari-vasara, the day of the Lord. Devotees avoid grains and beans, reduce eating and sleeping, and increase their chanting, reading and hearing about Krishna. The purpose is not hunger for its own sake. By simplifying the body's demands for one day, we free time and attention for remembering the Lord.

Srila Prabhupada recommended that devotees observe Ekadashi by fasting from grains and beans and by chanting more. Those who are able may fast more strictly; others can take simple non-grain foods. The essential thing is to dedicate the day to the Lord.

How to Observe Indira Ekadashi

  • Avoid grains, pulses and beans for the entire day: rice, wheat, dal, and foods made from them. Check packaged foods and spice mixes too; commercial asafoetida (hing), for example, is usually blended with wheat or rice flour. Fruits, milk products, nuts, potatoes and many vegetables are acceptable.
  • Increase your chanting. If you chant 16 rounds daily, try to add more. If you are new, chant whatever you can with attention.
  • Read or hear about the Lord. The story of Indrasena, or the Bhagavatam's description of Prahlada's prayers, is especially fitting today.
  • Remember your ancestors in prayer. Pray to Krishna for the welfare of departed family members and dedicate the spiritual benefit of your fast to them.
  • Feed others with prasadam. Offering food to the Lord and distributing it, especially in the name of departed relatives, is a meaningful act during this fortnight.
  • Break the fast correctly. On the following morning, break the fast with grains offered to Krishna within the prescribed parana time.

A note for families observing Pitru Paksha

Many readers will also be performing shraddha rites for their ancestors during this fortnight. A simple and spiritually powerful way to do this is to offer the food first to Lord Krishna and then distribute it as prasadam. Sponsoring prasadam distribution in the name of a departed loved one carries the same principle: whatever is connected with Krishna becomes eternally beneficial.

A Season to Turn Grief Into Service

Loss is one of the hardest experiences in life. The Bhagavad-gita (Bg. 2.13) gently reminds us that the soul does not die with the body, but continues its journey. Indira Ekadashi offers a positive response to grief: instead of only mourning, we can act. Every round of chanting, every act of service and every plate of prasadam shared in the name of our loved ones is a real offering that reaches them.
