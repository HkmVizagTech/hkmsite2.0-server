Few festivals light up India quite like Diwali. Homes are cleaned and painted, rangoli designs appear on doorsteps, sweets are exchanged between neighbours, and rows of lamps glow on every balcony in Visakhapatnam. For many families it is the biggest celebration of the year.

For devotees of Krishna, Diwali falls in the middle of Kartik, the holiest month of the Vaishnava calendar. That gives the festival a special depth. This article looks at the spiritual roots of Diwali, how Vaishnavas understand the worship of Lakshmi, and how to celebrate in a way that pleases the Lord.

The Lamps of Ayodhya

The best-known reason for Diwali is the return of Lord Ramachandra to Ayodhya. After fourteen years of exile in the forest, the abduction of Sita Devi and the great battle in Lanka, the Lord returned to His capital with Sita, Lakshmana, Hanuman and His allies. The Srimad-Bhagavatam (SB 9.10) describes how the city welcomed Him: the streets were washed and decorated, the citizens came out carrying offerings, and Bharata, who had ruled only as the Lord's servant, came forward with Rama's sandals on his head to return them.

Tradition remembers that the people of Ayodhya lit lamps throughout the city to welcome their king home. Every lamp we light on Diwali echoes that welcome.

Welcoming the Lord into the heart

The deeper meaning is easy to see. Ayodhya without Rama was a city in mourning, however beautiful its buildings. In the same way, a life without God, however comfortable, remains incomplete. The cleaning of homes before Diwali reminds us of the cleaning of the heart that is needed to welcome the Lord. Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu called the chanting of the Holy Name ceto-darpana-marjanam, the cleansing of the mirror of the heart (Siksashtakam 1, CC Antya 20.12).

Krishna's Victory Over Narakasura

In many parts of India, especially in the South, the day before Diwali is celebrated as Naraka Chaturdashi. The Srimad-Bhagavatam (SB 10.59) narrates how Lord Krishna, riding on Garuda with Satyabhama, attacked the fortress of the demon Bhaumasura, also called Narakasura. The demon had stolen the earrings of Aditi and the umbrella of Varuna and held more than sixteen thousand princesses captive. Krishna killed him with His disc and freed the princesses, who all prayed to have Him as their husband.

This pastime adds another layer to the festival. Light conquers darkness not by human effort alone, but because the Lord personally intervenes to protect His devotees and restore what belongs to Him.

Lakshmi Puja: The Vaishnava Understanding

On Diwali night, many families worship Goddess Lakshmi, praying for prosperity in the coming year. Vaishnavas worship Lakshmi with great reverence, but with one important understanding: Lakshmi is never alone. She is the eternal consort of Lord Narayana, and her greatest pleasure is to serve Him.

Srila Prabhupada often illustrated this with Ravana. Ravana wanted Sita, who is Lakshmi herself, but without Rama. He tried to take the goddess of fortune for his own enjoyment, and it destroyed him. The lesson for every household is clear: wealth that is used in the service of Lakshmi-Narayana brings peace and blessing, while wealth grabbed for selfish enjoyment brings anxiety and eventually loss.

Lakshmi serves the Lord's feet

The Brahma-samhita (Bs. 5.29) describes the spiritual realm, where hundreds of thousands of goddesses of fortune serve Lord Krishna with great respect and affection. If we want the blessings of Lakshmi, the most effective prayer is to ask for the opportunity to serve her Lord. Where Narayana is worshipped, Lakshmi naturally stays.

Diwali in the Month of Damodar

Diwali falls during Kartik, which ISKCON calendars show as running from 26 October to 24 November in 2026. This is the month when devotees offer ghee lamps every evening to Lord Damodar, child Krishna who was bound by mother Yashoda. On Diwali, the household lamps and the Damodar lamp come together beautifully. Many devotees make a special effort to offer a lamp to the Lord at the temple on this evening, and to light their homes in His honour rather than simply for decoration.

Close on the heels of Diwali comes Govardhan Puja, listed in ISKCON calendars for 10 November 2026, when Krishna lifted Govardhan Hill to protect the residents of Vrindavan. The festival season flows naturally from the lamps of Diwali to the mountain of food offered at Annakut.

Celebrating Diwali the Krishna-Conscious Way

Light lamps for the Lord

Begin your Diwali by offering a lamp to Krishna on your home altar, singing the Damodarashtakam or chanting the maha-mantra. Then light the rest of the house as an extension of that offering.

Offer sweets before sharing them

Diwali sweets become prasadam when they are first offered to the Lord. Prepare them without onion, garlic, eggs or other non-vegetarian ingredients, offer them with a simple prayer, and then distribute them to family, friends and neighbours.

Avoid what darkens the heart

Some Diwali customs, such as gambling, run against the regulative principles taught by Srila Prabhupada, who advised devotees to avoid gambling, intoxication, meat-eating and illicit relationships. A Krishna-conscious Diwali can be full of joy without these. Card games for money can be replaced with kirtan, storytelling and prasadam feasts.

Remember those who have little

For many families in our city, Diwali is a reminder of what they cannot afford. Sharing prasadam with those in need turns the festival of lights into a festival of compassion.

Tell the stories to children

Gather the children and narrate Lord Rama's return, Krishna's victory over Narakasura and Yashoda binding Damodar. These stories give meaning to the fireworks and lamps that children already love.
